### Core Principles

1. **Liberty**
   - **Definition**: The right of individuals to be free from oppression by the state.
   - **Historical Context**: Enlightenment philosophers like John Locke (who influenced both the Declaration of Independence and the U.S. Constitution) emphasized natural rights such as life, liberty, and property.
   - **Practical Implementation**: The First Amendment protects freedom of speech, religion, press, peaceful assembly, and petition to government; the Fifth Amendment prohibits self-incrimination and requires just compensation for takings of private property.
   - **Modern Relevance**: Liberty is a cornerstone in contemporary debates on privacy rights, gun control laws, and access to healthcare.

2. **Democracy**
   - **Definition**: Governance by the people through their elected representatives or direct participation.
   - **Historical Context**: Thomas Paine's "Common Sense" (1776) and James Madison’s Federalist Papers both played significant roles in shaping American democracy, advocating for a strong federal government to protect individual rights and freedoms.
   - **Practical Implementation**: The electoral process outlined in the Constitution ensures that representatives are elected by popular vote; the Bill of Rights protects citizens from potential tyranny while ensuring their voices are heard through voting and protest.
   - **Modern Relevance**: Democracy is continuously evolving, with advancements such as the Voting Rights Act (1965) and changes to campaign finance regulations reflecting its ongoing relevance.

3. **Federalism**
   - **Definition**: A system of government in which power is divided between a central authority and constituent political units.
   - **Historical Context**: Montesquieu’s concept of separation of powers influenced the balance of power among legislative, executive, and judicial branches; John Locke's ideas on property rights also played a part.
   - **Practical Implementation**: The Constitution establishes that federal government has specific enumerated powers while states retain sovereignty over non-enumerated areas. The Tenth Amendment explicitly reserves any powers not granted to the federal government to states or the people.
   - **Modern Relevance**: Federalism continues to be a critical issue today, especially in debates about healthcare reform (e.g., Medicaid expansion), immigration policy, and environmental regulation.

### Historical Context

Each of these principles emerged from a complex interplay between Enlightenment ideals, European colonial experiences, and early American political thought. Philosophers like John Locke’s works on natural rights inspired many founding documents while the British monarchy's heavy-handed approach to governance provided context for advocating against similar authoritarian structures.

### Practical Implementation in Constitutional Documents

- **U.S. Constitution**: The structure of government laid out in this document reflects a balance between centralized authority and decentralized power as articulated by federalism principles.
- **Bill of Rights**: This addition to the Constitution specifically addresses civil liberties, including protections from arbitrary imprisonment (Fifth Amendment), freedom of speech and religion (First Amendment), and other key rights.

### Modern Relevance

Each founding principle remains integral to American governance today. The evolving nature of these principles is evident in contemporary issues like judicial interpretation of laws that impact individual freedoms or the role of state versus federal governments in various policy areas.
